Engineering Design Process
Engineering is known as the art of designing something to produce a desired result.
However, designing a solution to a problem
cannot be done in just one step, rather, it
requires numerous levels of research,
planning, and testing in order to obtain a
satisfactory end product. This goes not just for
the overall design but also each individual
aspect of it. In order to streamline this process,
we use the Engineering Design Process to
guide us in our design process.
The Engineering Design Process is an iterative cycle that splits the challenging task of
developing a solution to a problem into a series of small steps that gradually get closer to a
solution. This process is used by all types of engineers, including architects, computer
scientists, automotive engineers, and scientists.
```
In the VEX Robotics Competition (VRC), the Engineering Design Process can be used to
```
create an effective robot to meet all the requirements of the game. By first identifying and
researching the problem, then brainstorming and selecting solutions, then building and
testing and evaluating those solutions, each individual aspect of the robot can be refined
and developed to match the criteria needed.
We will use the Engineering Design Process in order to develop our robot this year. This will
be documented clearly in our notebook with each step of the design process clearly
detailed and labeled. The step of the design process we are in will be clearly indicated on
the pages in this notebook.

Identify & Research Problem
1. The first step of the Engineering Design Process is identifying and researching the
problem. In this step, the constraints of the problem are laid out completely. Then,
the problem can be researched to see how it has been approached in the past.
Brainstorm Solutions
2. The second step of the Engineering Design Process is to brainstorm possible
solutions that match the specific criteria of the problem. It is common to use
prototypes, sketches, and CAD models to assist in this step. Normally, several
possible solutions should be considered in this step.
Select Solution
3. The third step is to select a particular solution out of the ones considered in the
previous step. The pros and cons of each solution will be weighed and then one will
be selected based on what is deemed most beneficial. However, this isn’t a final
solution, and this step can be repeated later if the selected solution isn’t satisfactory.
Design & Build Solution
4. The selected solution will now be designed and built. Normally, it will be modeled
with CAD onto the current robot.
Test Solution
5. After the solution is built, it will be tested to check if it meets the various criteria.
Depending on its performance, it may be fine tuned or modified to improve its
performance, or it may be scrapped and a new cycle of the design process will start.

rankings.

Important Terms & Definitions
1. Holding: A Robot status; A Robot is considered to be Holding if it meets any of the
following criteria during a Match:
- Trapping - Limiting the movement of an opponent Robot to one field tile or less. If the
robot is not attempting to escape then this is not holding.
- Pinning - Preventing the movement of an opponent Robot through contact with the
Field Perimeter, a Field Element, or another Robot.
- Lifting - Controlling an opponent’s movements by raising or tilting the opponent’s
Robot off of the foam tiles.
Robots may not hold other robots for more than 5 seconds in a match. After a hold is over, a
5 second cooldown must pass before the robot can begin holding again. A hold will be over
on one of the following conditions:
- The two Robots are separated by at least two (2) feet (approximately one foam tile).
- Either Robot has moved at least two (2) feet away (approximately one tile) from the
```
location where the Trapping or Pinning count began. (If lifting, this is measured from
```
```
where the lift ended.)
```
- The Holding Robot becomes Trapped or Pinned by a different Robot. i. In this case,
the original count would end, and a new count would begin for the newly Trapped or
Pinned Robot.
- In the case of Trapping, if an avenue of escape becomes available due to changing
circumstances in the Match.
2. Entanglement: A Robot status. A Robot is Entangled if it has grabbed, hooked, or
attached to an opposing Robot or a Field Element.
3. Violation: Violation - The act of breaking a rule in the Game Manual.
- Minor Violation: An accidental/momentary violation that results in a warning but no
disqualification.
- Major Violation - A violation which results in a Disqualification, including most Match
Affecting violations.
- Match Affecting - A violation which changes the winning and losing Alliance in the
Match.

as there was in VRC High Stakes or Tipping Point.

Game Element Analysis: Goals
There are 2 different types of Goals in VRC Push Back.
Firstly, there are Long Goals. There are 2 Long Goals in
a Push Back field. Long Goals are 48.79 inches long,
which is about 2 field tiles worth of space. Then, there
are two Center Goals, one Upper and one Lower. These
are 22.6 inches long, slightly shorter than a field tile.
Goal Size: Although the shapes and sizes of the Goals
vary, they all have more than enough space to store
Blocks without any resistance. The Upper Goal and the
Long Goals have a similar, diamond shape, while the Lower Goal has a
pentagonal shape. The Long Goal can store 15 Blocks, while the Center
Goals can hold about 7. However, the Long Goal also has a 13.33 inch
enclosed section in the middle.
Descoring Capability: As seen in a video by team 355Z, Goals can be
descored from quite easily. In fact, a mere push from a robot could
easily push out all the Blocks scored in a Goal, meaning that a robot
could easily do a last second descore. Additionally, as demonstrated in
a video by 56000A, a Goal can be de-scored completely by the
extension of 2 pneumatic cylinders. The low friction and large width of
the Goals allows for this ease of descoring.
Goal Height: The Long Goal and the High Center Goal are at two different heights, with the
Long Goal being elevated 12.5 inches from the ground while the High Center Goal is only
elevated 8 inches from the ground. Since the High Center Goal isn’t closed from the top
though, Blocks could potentially be dropped into it instead. This will require either a
mechanism that can score in both Goals, perhaps by dropping it into the High Center Goal
and pushing it with a ramp.

defense and light scoring, due to the defensive nature of this game, so it’s extremely
important to win the Autonomous Bonus in this game.

Game Challenge Question
Robot Design Question: What is a robot design that will be able to consistently and
efficiently score over 70 points in the early season and over 120 points in the late season?
What strategies will be able to achieve this score, and what designs on the robot are
necessary to execute these strategies? Additionally, how can we carry out these strategies
in the midst of the opposing alliance playing defense on ours? Lastly, how can we ensure
that our robot will effectively compete in the Robot Skills section of the VRC Push Back
game?
```
Solution: To achieve a sufficient score to guarantee the win in both early and late season,
```
our robot will need to be able to make use of all scoring methods available. If even a single
scoring method is unavailable, such as a certain Goal not being scorable with our robot or if
we can’t Park, we won’t be able to achieve the “Magic Score” to win the game.
In the following section of this Notebook, we will discuss numerous different strategies that
can be used to achieve the Magic Score. This includes:
1. Autonomous Strategies
a. Since the Autonomous Bonus is a key component of this year's score
distribution, it will be necessary to win the Autonomous Bonus.

C-Channel Boxing
Boxing is typically applied to almost every
structural joint on a robot whenever feasible. It is
done to improve the structural integrity of 2
c-channels attached via joints, and is typically done
when we’re joining flanges of c-channels. Boxing
```
uses longer screws and spacers (or nuts) to
```
distribute any force to the entire C-channel rather
than just the flange. This, in turn, gives us a stronger
and more reliable connection between c-channels.
There are two types of boxing — screw boxing and spacer boxing. Screw boxing, as seen in
the first image, uses a unique arrangement of keps nuts, whilst spacer boxing, of course,
uses spacers.
The idea is that the spacers or keps nut prevent the c-channel flanges from collapsing on
themselves when we begin to tighten our screws. This, in turn, allows us to distribute any
force to the entire c-channel rather than just the flange, increasing structural integrity on any
joint.
Triangle Bracing
Triangles, in general, are the strongest
shape of all. They have excellent
properties that distribute stress or force
very well. An example of their use is truss
bridges, where multiple triangles are
stacked on top of each other to create a
robust superstructure. We can apply triangles anywhere that is “under-supported.” The CAD
image above shows us using triangle-bracing to reinforce our three-wide back plate for
more structural integrity — this was primarily because they were under-supported. Even
though the joined c-channels are boxed, there is only one point of contact for each of the
four c-channels. Thus, triangle bracing was required to strengthen and further distribute any
impact force we may receive during gameplay to our inner c-channels.

Session #13 – Program: Goals & Approaches
Overview
One of the most important aspects of a VRC robot is to have a well-developed program to
use the robot. Code allows the robot to perform autonomous routines and automate tasks
that otherwise would have taken time and effort from the driver to do. It can also
occasionally fix certain mechanical issues on the robot. This potential shows the necessity of
having a competent program for our robot.
One of our goals this year was to create a modular and adaptable program which would run
well for any robot with minimal changes needed. This means there should be a high level of
abstraction so that the code can carry out complex functions without requiring many extra
lines of code.
Goals
1. Accuracy - The defining aspect of a good robot’s software is that it should do what it
is told to do with a high level of accuracy. If the robot is told to rotate 45 degrees, it
shouldn’t have more than a degree of error. Specifically, our goal for our code is that
all robot actions should have less than ±2.5% error when executed.
2. Consistency - A good robot program should also be consistent enough that the robot
does the exact same action every time a certain command is called. If the robot is
told to drive forward 5 inches, it should drive forward 5 inches every single time with
minimal error. This should take into account mechanical errors such as wheel slop,
bumps in the field, and, to an extent, interference from other robots & objects.
However, as it is impossible to have a perfectly consistent robot code, our goal is to
achieve an accurate robot action at least 90% of the time.
3. Convenience - One important aspect of robot software is the ease of use it provides
to the robot. Features like interactive screens on the brain, buttons doing multiple
actions at once, and automating tasks like color filtering can help the driver focus on
more important problems at hand. Generally, the more tasks are automated the
better the software is. Our goal is to develop a convenient brain screen and
automate every task which has the potential to be automated on the robot.

Purpose of a Control Algorithm
Although the VEX API provides functions for rotating motors a specific amount of degrees,
these functions often aren’t sophisticated enough to account for different weights and sizes
of robots and the different lengths and speeds that must be traveled. Oftentimes, the built in
VEX API functions will overshoot or undershoot the desired outcome. For this reason, it
becomes necessary to create a custom control algorithm – a set of instructions to manage
and regulate a process. Specifically, we need a control algorithm for our drivebase for both
driving certain distances and turning specific angles. However, we don’t need control
algorithms for the other subsystems of our robot like the intake and outtake as those simply
need to run at max speed at all times, programming control algorithms for them would be
an unnecessary use of the brain’s computing power.
PID Concept
```
One control algorithm known as PID (Proportional, Integral, Derivative) is a simple control
```
algorithm which can achieve high levels of accuracy with the correct tuning. It continuously
```
calculates an output voltage to the robot’s drive motors based on the current position (P),
```
```
cumulative error (I), and speed (D) of the robot. These values are multiplied by constants (kP,
```
```
kI, kD) in order to achieve a smooth transition from one point to another. However, these
```
constants must be precisely tuned, as incorrectly tuned constants can lead to the problems
```
below:
```
Constant Too High Too Low
kP Robot oscillates Robot is extremely slow
kI Robot overshoots Robot undershoots
kD Slow acceleration Robot continues oscillating
With proper tuning however, the proportional term of the algorithm will power the motors
early on in the motion, while the integral term will provide the power needed later on in the
motion when the robot is approaching its target. Meanwhile, the derivative term smooths out
the motion and reduces oscillation.

Purpose of a Position Tracking Algorithm
Whilst we now have PID controllers in place for high-accuracy control, certain obstacles
during a controlled drive motion may throw the robot off entirely. Competition factors, such
as another robot slightly bumping into ours or even a game object, can offset the robot’s
planned trajectory during a routine. This is where position tracking, or more commonly
known as odometry, comes into play. The robot maintains a live approximation of its position
on the field, enabling real-time adjustments during autonomous routines. Furthermore,
odometry provides greater consistency whilst programming and running routines.
Odometry/Position-Tracking Theory
Odometry uses robot encoders to approximate the robot's live position. These sensors can
```
be literally anything that returns a relative measurement to the robot. Examples include (but
```
```
are not limited to) distance sensors, LiDAR, vision, and rotational encoders. In VEX V5, the
```
most applicable sensors are distance and rotation-based. For our odometry system, we will
be using the V5 rotational sensors in addition to the V5 Motor Encoders in our software.
Ideally, we would grab a sample measurement from the sensors, compare it to a previous
```
measurement, determine the change (delta), and then output an approximation for the
```
robot's position.
Our implementation approach is relatively similar to most teams. Our
drivetrain encoders act as our vertical translation, whilst there is a dead
horizontal wheel acting as horizontal translation to account for drift.
There are two approximation types to consider as well: linear and arc.
Linear models the robot's motion as a straight line, whilst arc models it as an arc of a circle.
Arc approximations are better for a simple reason: the robot never moves in a truly straight
line. Therefore, modeling the robot's motion in this way will reduce the odometry system's
accumulated error over time. Since the robot never moves in a true straight line, linear
approximations will, in theory, accumulate more error from the robot's motion.
