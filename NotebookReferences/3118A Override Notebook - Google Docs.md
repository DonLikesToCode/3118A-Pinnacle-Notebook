clear navigation and descriptions of each page. There will be multiple sections of our

###### **Identify & Research Problem** 

1.  The first step of the Engineering Design Process is identifying and researching the problem. In this step, the constraints of the problem are laid out completely. Then, the problem can be researched to see how it has been approached in the past. 

###### **Brainstorm Solutions** 

2.  The second step of the Engineering Design Process is to brainstorm possible solutions that match the specific criteria of the problem. It is common to use prototypes, sketches, and CAD models to assist in this step. Normally, several possible solutions should be considered in this step. 

###### **Select Solution** 

3.  The third step is to select a particular solution out of the ones considered in the previous step. The pros and cons of each solution will be weighed and then one will be selected based on what is deemed most beneficial. However, this isn’t a final 

   - solution, and this step can be repeated later if the selected solution isn’t satisfactory. 

###### **Design & Build Solution** 

4.  The selected solution will now be designed and built. Normally, it will be modeled with CAD onto the current robot. 

###### **Test Solution** 

5.  After the solution is built, it will be tested to check if it meets the various criteria. Depending on its performance, it may be fine tuned or modified to improve its performance, or it may be scrapped and a new cycle of the design process will start.

##### **Notable Rules** 

While many rules are ordinary, some unique & relevant rules are explained below. 

###### **<u>Specifc Game Rules (<SG>)</u>** 

**<SG1>, <SG2> & <SG3>** all set the rules for expansion  in VRC Override. Although the robot must start in an 18” x 18” x 18” volume, it can expand to a 24” x 24” area horizontally and up to **50 inches upwards.** This is significant because  it means robots can likely stack up to 7 Pins on a single Goal. In order to remain competitive, robots must be able to quickly move Pins from the field tiles to a height of 30-50 inches. This will require some sort of efficient lift. 

**<SG6>** states that robots can only possess 1 Cup and  1 Pin at once. If a robot is possessing more than that, it must immediately remove the possession of those objects. This is significant because robots will not need to be capable of storing multiple Cups and Pins which saves space (in fact, they should minimize their storage space to minimize the risk of violating this rule). Additionally, it increases the importance of a fast lifting mechanism since Cups and Pins must all be stacked individually. 

**<SG7> & <SG8>** explain the limits of the Autonomous  Period. Robots may not cross the Autonomous Line, though they can use the Midfield. Game elements on the Autonomous Line can be used with caution as it may lead to interaction with the other team. Allowing the Midfield to be used in the Autonomous Period could lead to rush strategies where both teams race to control the tall Goal. 

**<SG9>** states that Alliance Goals can only be interacted  with by the Alliance that the Goal belongs to. This means that the Alliance Goals can likely be left empty until later on in the match. This could allow us to score different Pins on it based on the current situation of the score - if a team was losing by a lot they could score yellow/yellow Pins in hopes of claiming those points and winning the game, while if an alliance is already winning, they would want to just score Pins of their own color to avoid the risk of losing the game due to a last-second toggle flip. This rule also means the driver must be careful not to drive by the opposing Alliance’s Goals to prevent accidentally touching them.

**<SG10>** states that descoring is illegal from neutral or the opposing Alliance’s Goals. In Appendix C, it is further stated that violations will be considered Match Affecting if the offending Alliance wins by 15 points or less. This rule is quite significant because it makes the robots focus on scoring speed instead of overly defensive strategies. It also means that the driver must be quite precise so that they don’t accidentally knock over Pins from the neutral Goals. 

**<SG12>** lays out the rules for the Endgame period.  In these last 10 seconds of the match, robots inside the Midfield can’t extend more than 18” high. It also makes defense a bit more lenient, saying that incidental damage won’t be considered a violation of <GG14>. The height limit is especially relevant because it essentially illegalizes scoring on the tall Goal during the Endgame. 

###### **<u>Scoring Rules</u>** 

(The rules below relate to strategy while the next page will detail scoring conditions) 

**<SC2>** explains the criteria for a Pin to be considered  Placed. More importantly though, it clarifies that robot contact doesn’t affect the Pin’s status. 

**<SC5>** explains how the yellow Pins will be considered  scored, stating that their points will go to the Alliance that owns the Toggle of that quadrant or has more robots in the Midfield. If there are an equal amount of robots in the Midfield, the yellow Pins in the midfield won’t count for either Alliance. This means that scores can flip rapidly as a single Toggle switch or robot movement could change the score by dozens of points. 

**<SC6>** states that a Robot will be considered in the  Midfield as long as **any part of the robot is in the 3D projection** of the Midfield.  This means that a robot could likely drive to the Midfield in the last second in order to gain those points. 

**<SC7>** lays out the Autonomous Win Point criteria.  An Alliance must have 7 Pins placed in their side of the Field, 3 Goals with 2 Pins scored for their Alliance, and no robots contacting the field perimeter. Due to limits on Possession from <SG6>, this will be difficult for a single team to do and will likely require significant coordination between Alliances.
